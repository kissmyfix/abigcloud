# **The Title-Transfer Mechanism**
Tennessee's constitution (Article II, § 28) prohibits governments from simply waiving a company's property taxes. The workaround runs through ownership: government-owned property is automatically tax-exempt, and an [Industrial Development Board](/explainers/what-is-an-idb/) is legally "a public instrumentality" of its city, so property the IDB owns pays no tax ([T.C.A. § 7-53-305](/sources/state_of_tennessee/tn_annotated_code/tca-7-53-305-tax-exemption-pilot.pdf)).

The mechanism, step by step:

1. The company (or a landowner) transfers title of the property to the IDB, typically by **quitclaim deed for $0**. No money changes hands; only the name on the deed changes.
2. The IDB, now the owner of tax-exempt property, **leases it back to the company**, which builds and operates on the land exactly as if it owned it.
3. The company pays the negotiated [PILOT](/explainers/what-is-a-pilot/) amounts instead of taxes for the agreement's term.
4. At the end of the term, title transfers back and the property returns to the tax rolls.

On paper, the county assessor shows a $0 tax obligation because the parcel belongs to a public instrumentality. In practice the "landlord" is a formality: in documented Gallatin cases, the assessor's mailing address for the IDB-owned parcel routes to the tenant. For the Meta site, that address is Meta's headquarters in Menlo Park, California.

**Why it matters in this investigation:** the same $0-quitclaim-and-leaseback sequence appears in the [2014 Beretta records](/sources/state_of_tennessee/tn_property_assessments/sumner-assessment-beretta.pdf) and the [2020 Woolhawk deal](/sources/state_of_tennessee/tn_property_assessments/sumner-assessment-woolhawk.pdf). It is the IDB's standing playbook, and every dollar in the case flows through it.

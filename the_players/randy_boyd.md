# Randy Boyd (Randall Boyd)

**Role in this story:** Tennessee's Commissioner of Economic and Community Development, then President of the University of Tennessee — the office that controlled the trust land Project Woolhawk needed.

## Why he is in scope

The Woolhawk site required assembling 800+ acres, none of it city-owned. Among the parcels was a large farm held **in a trust** whose terms directed the land to the **University of Tennessee for agricultural experimentation**. Amending that trust took, in James Fenton's telling, **61 votes** — 24 of them county commissioners.

UT initially refused. Fenton says he had lunch with then-president **Joe DiPietro**, who was encouraging at first ("we do that all the time, we've even got somebody on staff that sells"), then wrote back within weeks: *"sorry, somebody has had their eyes on that for quite a while."* Fenton: *"at that point it was a possibility of the whole thing going sideways."*

Then, in Fenton's own words on the Growing Pains podcast (Sumner County Republican Freedom Caucus):

> "However, through a lot of conversations, as it turned out, **Randall Boyd, who was commissioner of economic development for Tennessee was now president of UT**. **They had just reduced the size of their board.** And **one of our elected officials had had the chance to interview all of them before they got selected**. And so there were a lot of connections there. **The board of trustees eventually did sign off and say that you could sell that property.**"

Source: `podcasts/transcripts/fenton-on-podcast-full.txt`, approx. 00:12:50–00:13:20.

## What this establishes and what it does not

**Establishes:** the state's chief industrial recruiter held the UT presidency at the moment UT's board reversed course on land a data center project needed; the board had recently been reduced in size; a local elected official had interviewed incoming trustees before their selection; and the participant who assembled the deal describes these facts together, as the reason it went through.

**Does not establish:** that Boyd did anything improper, or anything at all. Fenton's account is a *sequence*, not an allegation, and no document in the project shows Boyd acting on the Woolhawk parcel. Nothing here should characterize his conduct.

**This is why the entry can carry weight without a Knoxville investigation.** The project's core thesis is that the system is built so laws do not have to be broken. This episode is that thesis in miniature: a restrictive trust blocks the deal, the university says no, the leadership and the board change, the university says yes. Every step has an innocent explanation available and the obstacle dissolves regardless. The demonstration does not require a bad actor.

## Open verification (cheap, do before quoting)

1. **When was the UT Board of Trustees reduced, and by what instrument?** If a 2018 act of the General Assembly restructured the board, that is a second statute inside the Woolhawk window (alongside Public Chapter 265, April 2019) and is checkable from public legislative records.
2. **When did Boyd become UT president, and in what capacity** (interim vs. permanent), relative to DiPietro's refusal and the board's approval? The sequence only means what Fenton implies if the dates line up as he tells them.
3. **Which elected official interviewed the trustees before selection?** Fenton does not name them. This is the load-bearing name in the paragraph and the only genuinely new lead in it.

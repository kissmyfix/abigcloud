INTRODUCTION

The City of Gallatin’s Industrial Development Board has engaged Studio Eight Design
to prepare an updated Space Needs Assessment to help evaluate and plan for the
future of Gallatin’s City Hall. The City is in discussions with Boyle Investment
Company to explore the feasibility of developing a new mixed-use development in
downtown Gallatin. The proposed development will consider transforming two
city-owned blocks into a dynamic live/work corridor featuring a major hotel brand,
residential units, open green spaces, retail and restaurant space and possibly a new
City Hall to meet the needs of the community.
The goal of this Space Needs Assessment is twofold:
1. To help determine the range of square footage required if the city decides to build
a new City Hall, and
2. To help determine whether it is feasible to renovate the existing City Hall to
accommodate current and future space needs. If the existing building is renovated,
the space needs assessment will illustrate how long the future needs can be
accommodated within the facility.
A preliminary probable opinion of cost will be provided for these two scenarios,
based on current regional construction costs for similar facilities. This report will
support the City’s due diligence effort to determine whether to pursue a public-
private partnership with Boyle as part of the proposed mixed-use development.
KEY CONCULSIONS
In a perfect world, the space needs assessment determined a need @ 47,000 gross
square feet in twenty years. The above square footage needs does not take into
account actual floor plates or possible stacking scenarios for the site chosen.
Based on the required square footage, the building will most likely be a 2-story
building. Therefore, the grossing factor of 25% used within the assessment included
square footage for vertical circulation elements such as stairs and elevators. If City
Hall remains downtown, a two-story building is in keeping with the height, scale, and
massing of historic downtown Gallatin.
The existing building consists of two levels above grade with one basement level
totaling @ 37,000 square feet. Based on the results of the assessment, the square
footage of the existing building does not meet the current and future square footage
needs for the departments currently located within City Hall.
If the city decides to proceed with the construction of a new facility, consideration
may be given to inclusion of a basement to meet additional storage needs, potentially
provide a storm shelter, and provide additional growth and / or storage space for
the future. A storm shelter is not currently included in the space needs assessment.

                                                                   INTRODUCTION

                      GALLATIN’S PRESENT CITY HALL
                      The existing City Hall structure was constructed in 1974. The City commissioned
                      a Building Assessment in 2020 which concluded the following:
                      • The building could operate for an additional 10+ years (up to year 2030).
                      After that time, it will be increasingly difficult to find replacement parts for the
                      mechanical and electrical systems requiring an extensive and costly replacement
                      of the building infrastructure.
                      • The building is not sprinklered and not in compliance with current building,
                      life safety codes, and accessibility codes.
                      • The walls of the central corridor are load bearing making modifications
                      to these walls difficult and potentially cost-prohibitive, limiting the ability to
                      extensively renovate the building.
                      • A security assessment should be conducted to determine if upgrades are
                      required to provide the necessary safety for staff and the visitors to the building.
                      • The current layout of the interior is compartmentalized with multiple hallways,
                      concrete block walls, and very little access to daylight which is not reflective
                      of healthy work environments of the 21st century. Attracting new talent and
                      retaining current staff may become challenging given the state of the physical
                      work environment within the existing building.
                      • The current building lacks organizational clarity. A first-time visitor must seek
                      out the department they need if there is not a person at the front desk.
                      • Toilet rooms and the current elevator do not appear to meet current
                      accessibility codes. The Toilet Rooms and Elevator Shaft are landlocked
                      by surrounding spaces and load-bearing masonry, making expansion more
                      challenging and costly.
                      *In the responses from Department Directors, there were a few items listed
                      repeatedly by nearly all respondents:
                      a) More windows for natural light; b) More contiguous, flexible space to keep
                      staff within departments together rather than separated across halls, in different
                      rooms, or in separate areas of the building; c) More & different types of Conference
                      Space; d) A shared, common Staff Break Room Area; and e) Improved security.
                      The existing City Hall structure and layout presents challengesto address these
                      features within the limited space available. The cost of the renovation will
                      involve upgrades & replacement of building structure, infrastructure systems,
                      technology, and current accessibility codes. This will make these renovations
                      and any expansion more expensive than typical office space renovations. Based
                      upon our observations, the feedback from staff, and anticipated costs to remedy
                      base building systems in addition to creating a more efficient, welcoming, secure,
                      and functional City Hall, we believe that the current facility has lived it’s life and
                      served the City well, but a new facility may be a more prudent, cost-effective
                      long-term solution to address the next 20 years as Gallatin continues to grow.

                                                                         BACKGROUND

                      Gallatin, Tennessee was established on the Cumberland River and made the county
                      seat of Sumner County in 1802. It is located approximately 30.6 miles northeast of
                      the state capital of Nashville. The General Assembly of the State of Tennessee by
                      an act passed Nov. 6, 1801 appointed Samuel Wilson, Charles Donoho, and Thomas
                      Murray, commissioners to purchase 40 acres of land and lay out a town for the seat
                      of justice. On February 25, 1802 the commissioners purchased the original site of
                      the town of Gallatin from James Trousdale for $490, containing 42 and a half acres
                      including an acre reserved by Captain Trousdale and one and a half acres in the public
                      square.
                      The city was named for Albert Gallatin, United States Secretary of the Treasury to
                      Presidents Thomas Jefferson and James Madison. In 1803, the first county courthouse
                      and jail were built on the central town square. Andrew Jackson became one of the
                      first to purchase a lot when the town was surveyed and platted in 1803. The same
                      year, Andrew Jackson built a home and the first general store in Gallatin. Andrew
                      Jackson practiced law on the historic town square and in the Douglass Clark House,
                      which served as the first Courthouse in Sumner County.
                      In 1809, the original site of the city was increased by purchasing 29 acres of land
                      adjoining it to the east. Gallatin continued to be governed by the commissioners
                      until it was incorporated on Nov. 7, 1815. The early growth of the city was slow and
                      steady. In 1830, according to the Tennessee Gazetteer, the city contained, “a good
                      courthouse, jail and public offices, a large brick church used by all denominations, a
                      Masonic Home, a printing office, 12 stores, two taverns, 11 lawyers, four physicians,
                      one cabinet shop, one chair factory, three tailor shops, two shoemaker shops, two
                      saddle shops, one wagon maker, one tan yard, one tinner and coppersmith, three
                      blacksmith shops, one hatter, one male and two female academies, 666 inhabitants of
                      which 234 were black, 35 log, 38 frame, and 27 brick houses.”
                      In 1873 the population was devastated by a cholera epidemic, killing one out of every
                      fifteen people. The town was also devastated by multiple fires in the late 1800s.
                      Early downtown buildings were constructed of wood, contributing to the fires. As
                      fire codes were implemented, brick became more prevalent as seen in the historic
                      structures still in place throughout downtown today.
                      Today, Gallatin is the 5th fastest growing city in Tennessee. Over the past 3 Censuses,
                      Gallatin’s population has demonstrated significant growth:
                      • 2000 United States Census: Population of 23,230 people.
                      • 2010 United States Census: Population of 30,278 people.
                      • 2020 United States Census: Population of 44,431 people.
                      • As of July 1, 2022 the US Census estimates Gallatin’s population at 48,194 people.
                      • Gallatin anticipates growing to a population of 56,000 within the next 10 years.

BACKGROUND

Gallatin has a Mayor-Council government. The City Council is made up of seven
elected officials: five are elected from single-member districts within the city limits,
and two members are at-large. Of these seven council members, one is elected by
members of the council as Vice-Mayor to serve a limited term. Meetings are presided
over by the Mayor, who is elected at-large by voters of the city. The current Mayor
and Council Members are:
• Page Brown, Mayor                    • Craig Hayes, District 4
• Lynda Love, District 1               • Steven Carter, District 5
• Dr. Aaron Allison, District 2 • Shawn Fennell, Council at Large
•   Pascal Jouvence, District 3
Gallatin was named the #7 Best City to move to in Tennessee by USA Today in
2024. Several national companies have facilities or headquarters in Gallatin, including
Facebook (META), Gap, Inc., Beretta and Servpro Industries, Inc. Gallatin was formerly
the headquarters of Dot Records, originally founded to highlight local Tennessee
artists. The city is also the site of Volunteer State Community College, a two-year
college with more than 70 degreed programs. In 2017, Gallatin was ranked as “The
Nicest Place In America” by Reader’s Digest.
Since it was founded, Historic Downtown Gallatin, Inc., has spearheaded the
revitalization of Gallatin’s historic downtown core. The revitalization effort has
followed the four-point program of the National Main Street Center of the National
Trust for Historic Preservation: Organization, Design, Promotion and Economic
Restructuring. Working in partnership with city and county government, property
owners, preservationists, local businesses and merchants in the historic retail district,
Historic Downtown Gallatin, Inc. has helped create one of the nation’s best Main
Streets. Gallatin has earned recognition as an authentic, eclectic place that offers
something for everyone in an atmosphere that reminds locals and visitors alike of a
simpler time in history – a place where community matters.
Gallatin is growing and a desirable place to live. Downtown is undergoing a thriving
revitalization with a mix of community businesses. City Hall has always been a part of
the Downtown community, reinforcing the level of service the City offers businesses
and residents alike. Keeping City Hall downtown – whether through renovating the
existing structure or building a new City Hall as part of the new redevelopment is
to be commended. Maintaining a civic presence downtown reinforces the historic
district’s heritage and importance as a landmark for all of Gallatin.
Largely because of the pandemic, Gallatin is experiencing a significant growth
explosion in population as people from larger urban centers are relocating to Middle
Tennessee. This Space Needs Assessment will identify the anticipated growth in City
Staff to continue to deliver a high level of service to its residents and businesses as
Gallatin continues to grow.

                                              SUMMARY OF DOCUMENT

                      ASSESSMENT METHODOLOGY
                      Studio Eight Design began the space needs assessment process with a kickoff meeting
                      with City Leadership to review the organizational charts for each department and
                      to take a tour of the existing facility. Studio Eight Design then developed two
                      questionnaires, one for Leadership and one for each department to be completed
                      by the departmental directors. Studio Eight Design used the information gathered
                      from the initial kick off meeting and the questionnaires feedback to develop the
                      Space Needs Assessment.
                      The purpose of the space needs assessment is to provide a high-level estimation of
                      City Hall square footage to meet the current and future needs of a growing Gallatin
                      community. The study is intended to equip the City of Gallatin with data for long-
                      term decision making in the evaluation of a variety of development options. The
                      assessment is a benchmark and is in no way intended to reflect the square footage
                      of a final design for the facility. Site constraints can have an impact on the building
                      arrangement and stacking, leading to alterations in the overall building square footage.
                      ASSESSMENT OVERVIEW
                      City Halls are planned as long-term facilities that should serve a municipality for many
                      decades into the future, allowing for growth while facilitating efficiency in operations
                      over the lifespan of the building. You will find that we grouped departments according
                      to their adjacency needs. To maximize operational efficiency, departments that
                      interact most should be located spatially adjacent to each other. The zones listed
                      below consist of departments that should be adjacent to each other within the
                      building to maximize efficiency of space and City operations.
                      •   Administrative Zone:        Mayor and City Administration (Inclusive of
                          Communication offices), Attorney, Human Resources, Finance and Purchasing
                      •   Public Access Zone: City Clerk and Economic Development, Communications
                          (Production Studio)
                      •   Development Services Zone: Codes, Planning, Engineering
                      •   Facilities Support Zone: Information Technology and Facilities

SUMMARY OF DOCUMENT

To assist the city in the evaluation of options, the Assessment includes three different
Space Needs scenarios.
Option A: Space Needs based solely on the information gathered from the
Departmental Questionnaires
**Note: All workspace assignments, i.e. office vs. workstations, were made according to
the department directors’ request within the questionnaire. The assessment includes
recommendations for shared support spaces within the zones such as work rooms and
conference rooms.
Option B: Relocating Codes, Planning, and Facilities to the Public Works Facility
**Note: Operationally, Codes and Planning would benefit from being co-located with the
Engineering department. Currently, the Engineering Department is located offsite in the
Public Works Facility. This Option summarizes the reduction in City Hall square footage if
Codes and Planning were to move to the same facility as the Engineering Department. This
is a cost savings measure to the City Hall project, but the cost to renovate or add on to an
existing facility should be considered in the evaluation of this option.
The projected square footage is based upon developing and adopting space standards.
The space standards are modular building blocks that allow for maximum flexibility
for future growth. For example, an enclave (small conference room) is sized the
same as a private office so spaces can be converted over time by only changing
out the furniture and not reconfiguring the walls. Open office environments with
workstations provide maximum flexibility over time and allow spaces to adapt to
growth with minimal disruptions and expense. The spaces standards used for this
assessment are included in the document for reference.
Each departmental net square footage includes a 25% circulation factor. The overall
building gross square footage includes a 25% grossing factor for building core items
such as restrooms, stairs, elevators, mechanical, and electrical rooms. The 25%
grossing factor also includes square footage for exterior and interior wall thickness
and structural elements.

      OFFICE DESIGN RECOMMENDED BEST PRACTICES

                      RECOMMENDED BEST PRACTICES
                      Office trends change over time, but there are many best practices that should be
                      implemented in office design to maximize efficiency of operations and flexibility
                      over time and to promote health and well-being of employees. Some of these best
                      practices are listed below.
                      •   Maximize access to daylight: We recommend placing open office areas along
                          the building perimeter and locating the offices internal to the floor plate with
                          glass fronts facing the exterior wall. This maximizes access to daylight within the
                          workspaces for all building occupants. It also promotes better energy efficiency
                          and lower operational costs by utilizing more daylight throughout all spaces in
                          lieu of dependency on electric lights.
                      •   Maximize flexibility in the initial design to facilitate growth over the lifespan
                          of the building, i.e. limit construction of hard walls and maintain open office
                          environments where possible.
                      •   Implement technology that is user friendly and scalable over time.
                      •   Implement sound masking as a tool to minimize audible disruptions within an
                          open office environment.
                      •   Provide a variety of sizes of shared conference rooms.
                      •   Provide enclaves (small meeting rooms) that are equipped with the technology
                          for Teams/Zoom meetings to limit the number of virtual meetings taken from
                          the work areas.
                      •   Provide phone rooms or phone booths where those in an open office environment
                          can go to make a private phone call.
                      •   Provide shared amenity spaces between departments - coffee/break areas,
                          work areas, and waiting areas to allow for efficiency in operations and promote
                          collaboration amongst departments.
                      •   Provide conveniently located amenities such as wellness rooms, mother’s rooms,
                          and fitness center.
                      •   Promote movement through the office with conveniently located stairs, sit-stand
                          desks, and outdoor spaces accessible by employees.


                      In a well-designed City Hall, the spaces that the visitors most need should be most
                      accessible. A City Hall is divided into three distinct functional areas:
                      •       Formal Public Spaces such as the Council Chambers, multi-purpose rooms
                              and courts.
                      •       Service Areas: Recorder, Codes, Planning, Economic Development
                      •       Administrative and other Office Ares (Staff Zone)

CITY HALL DESIGN RECOMMENDED BEST PRACTICES

Having clear, secure delineation between the public zones and staff zones of the
building is critical to the safety of the building occupants. Below are recommended
security best practices for City Hall Design:
•   Maintain separation between the secure staff zones and public zones of the
    building. This is typically addressed through access-controlled points between
    the public zone and staff zones of the building.
•   Limit the number of access points into the building
•   Provide infrastructure for increased security needs over time (i.e. metal detectors
    and/or x-ray machines)
•   Maintain appropriate level of security at the services counters (The necessary
    level of security can be determined by a security consultant expert through the
    evaluation of a recent Crimecast Report)

OPINION OF PROBABLE COST




                      The costs below are ROM (“Rough Order of Magnitude”) opinions of cost based on
                      cost per square foot of similar projects recently completed by Studio Eight Design.
                      ROM estimates can vary as much as 50%. Therefore, it is recommended that a Pre-
                      Construction cost estimator is brought on board to provide more accurate pricing
                      as the City proceeds with the design process for renovation or construction of a new
                      City Hall.


                      City Halls are not the same building type as typical office buildings. They are built
                      to be Owner-Occupied for long-term use. The materials and systems factor in cost
                      efficiencies for maintenance, quality, and durability for longer periods than typical
                      office space. As a result, the cost / square foot is often higher and budget expectations
                      should be established accordingly. The City will want to clarify its role as “Owner” vs
                      “Tenant” in weighing its options of controlling the specifications and maintenance of
                      building systems, finishes, and operating costs.


                      Cost per square foot is for vertical buidling construction hard costs only. Cost does
                      not include site improvements or soft costs, such as design fees, FFE (furniture,
                      fixtures, equipment), permitting, insurance, etc.


                      Costs given are based on similar current 2024 project cost rates. We recommended
                      carrying a minimum of 3.5% - 4% annual inflation for years beyond.


                      We recommend carrying a minimum 5% contingency until more accurate design
                      based pricing can be acquired from a Pre-Construction cost estimator.

Here is exactly how this process operates mechanically, how the entities are structured, and how the funds are handled.

## 1. The "Loophole": Bypassing the Tennessee Constitution
The Tennessee Constitution explicitly mandates that all property must be taxed equally according to its value, and it strictly prohibits cities or counties from granting direct property tax abatements to private corporations. A city council cannot simply vote to give a large manufacturer a 0% property tax rate. [3, 4, 5] 
To bypass this restriction legally, the state legislature used a different constitutional carve-out: government-owned property is 100% tax-exempt. [5] 
By creating an Industrial Development Board (IDB) as a separate public corporation, the local government triggers a three-step legal maneuver: [1, 4, 6] 

   1. The Title Transfer: The private corporation transfers the legal deed/title of its land, factories, or equipment directly to the IDB.
   2. The Tax Disappearance: Because the IDB is a public entity, the property legally becomes government-owned. The standard property tax bill instantly drops to $0.
   3. The Lease-Back: The IDB leases the property back to the corporation for a nominal fee (often $1 or a basic administrative fee). [4, 5, 7, 8, 9] 

------------------------------
## 2. The 501(c) Misconception: Section 115 Entities
Where your research notes that these IDBs "become a 501(c)" non-profit, they are actually acting as a different type of tax-exempt entity entirely. [2, 10, 11] 
IDBs are legally structured as public, instrumentalities of the state or public benefit corporations. Their federal tax exemption does not come from IRS Section 501(c)(3) (which governs private charities). Instead, they operate under Internal Revenue Code Section 115(1), which governs income derived from essential governmental functions. [2, 6] 
They look and act like non-profits because: [2] 

* They have no private shareholders and cannot distribute profits.
* Their corporate bylaws explicitly use non-profit, public-benefit structuring language.
* They file corporate registration papers with the Tennessee Secretary of State. [2, 6, 12] 

------------------------------
## 3. How the PILOT Funds are Handled
Because the property tax has been wiped out by the IDB's ownership, the local government loses massive amounts of potential revenue. To offset this, the IDB negotiates a Payment in Lieu of Taxes (PILOT) agreement. [4, 5, 10] 
The mechanics of the PILOT cash flow follow a specific track:

[ Private Corporation ] 
       │
       ▼ (Pays Negotiated PILOT Fees; usually ~50% of normal tax rate)
[ Industrial Development Board (IDB) ]
       │
       ▼ (Distributes funds to cover bonds or administrative overhead)
[ Local City / County Treasury ]


* The Discount: The corporation agrees to pay a fractional amount of what their normal tax bill would have been (for example, 30% to 50%). This acts as an indirect tax abatement. [7, 9] 
* The Intermediary Role: The corporation writes the check to the IDB, not the standard county tax assessor. [5, 13] 
* Fund Allocation: The IDB acts as the clearinghouse. If the IDB issued Industrial Revenue Bonds (conduit debt) to help finance the factory's construction, some of the PILOT funds may be used to pay off those financial instruments. The remaining contractually obligated funds are then clawed back and distributed into the local city or county general treasury funds to support schools, infrastructure, or general government services. [5, 9, 10, 14, 15]

## Tracking and Auditing These Boards
Because IDBs wield immense power to strip property off local tax rolls, they are heavily monitored for transparency by the state. You can audit their activity through official government portals: [1, 10] 

* PILOT Disclosures: The [Tennessee Comptroller's SBOE PILOT Reporting Portal](https://comptroller.tn.gov/boards/state-board-of-equalization/sboe-services/property-tax-incentive-programs/pilot-reporting.html) mandates that companies leasing from IDBs file annual reports on their tax exemptions. [16] 
* Debt Tracking: You can view outstanding debt agreements and compliance audits for any specific local board through the [Tennessee Comptroller State Funding Board Guidelines](https://comptroller.tn.gov/office-functions/lgf/debt/industrial-development-corporations-debt-reporting.html). [1, 17]
